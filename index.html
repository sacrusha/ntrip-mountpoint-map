GPS can achieve centimetre accuracy — this map shows the free correction networks. how it works

Why your GPS reads in metres
GPS satellites broadcast timing signals. Atmospheric delays and other errors compound to 5–10 metres under typical conditions. The chip in your receiver is capable of much better — it just needs a correction signal.

Two routes to centimetre accuracy

Standalone correction receivers process satellite-delivered correction signals — no internet, no coverage map needed. Complete units with no ongoing fees (e.g. Galileo HAS receivers) start around $2,900 / €2,700. Subscription-dependent hardware exists from ~$850, but requires paid correction fees on top and typically won't work with free PPP services. Search for "PPP receiver" or "Galileo HAS" to explore those. This map isn't about them.

Network RTK — what this map is about — pairs a dual-band receiver with corrections streamed over the internet from a nearby reference station. Within 30 km of a station, centimetre accuracy is typical. If you already own a survey-grade or dual-frequency receiver, it likely supports this. Complete ready-to-use units start around €630 / $700; tinkerer kits from ~€275.

What the map shows
Each pin is a free public reference station. Click it for connection details. The colour zones show reliable range: green < 10 km (typically < 2 cm) · yellow-green 10–30 km · amber 30–50 km · pale red 50–100 km (decimetre range). Purple circles are virtual networks — one connection point, the network synthesises a nearby reference for you.
